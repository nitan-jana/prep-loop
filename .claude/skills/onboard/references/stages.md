# The stages

Four, in order. Each is a sitting of its own — resumable, and better split than
rushed. The rules in the skill file apply inside all of them and are not
repeated here.

Update the progress table in `instance/profile/README.md` at the end of every stage, not
at the end of the last one.

---

## Spine

*About forty-five minutes. Writes `identity`, `attribution`, `state`,
`schedule`. After this the system can run a day.*

**Enumerate first.** Repository list, resume, public profile text. Read all of
it before the first question.

**Ask what is out of bounds first.** Show the list, ask which entries must not
be read or recorded, and record the answer before writing anything. See
[`verifying.md`](verifying.md#ask-what-is-out-of-bounds-before-writing-anything).

**Open on what was found, not on the user.** Name the three or four
repositories with real history and ask them to walk through one. Attribution
comes out of that conversation naturally, and it has to be settled before any
claim is written down.

Push on:

- **Scope.** "The service" is doing a lot of work in most sentences. What part?
- **Who else.** Shared work described as solo is the claim most likely to be
  opened and the cheapest to fix now.
- **Numbers.** Every one gets checked or marked `stated`, in the moment.
- **The stack line.** Ask what happens when an interviewer opens each one up.
  The answer sorts the list into what can be defended and what cannot, which is
  more useful than any rating.

**`state.md` is the highest-value file in the profile.** Every moving fact
lands there and nowhere else — what phase the preparation is in, what triggers
the next one, what is deliberately paused and what unpauses it. A fact that
lives here once is a fact that cannot be right in two skills and stale in a
third.

**`schedule.md` captures cadence rather than assuming it.** Which days are
working days, how many blocks, how long, what usually sits in each, when the
three recurring sessions happen, the timezone, and both cap values. Ask; do not
propose a default and have it accepted out of politeness.

Ask what has actually been sustained before, not what would be ideal. A
schedule built from the ideal answer produces a week that is abandoned by the
middle of it, and then reads as a week of failures.

**Close by testing it.** Ask two questions back — a scope claim and a number —
and see whether the answers come from memory.

## Resources

*About thirty minutes. Writes `resources`, plus one inventory per source.*

Fully covered in [`catalogs.md`](catalogs.md). The short version: ask what is
already in use before recommending anything, record what was rejected and why,
and degrade acquisition down the tiers without ceremony.

## Material

*About forty-five minutes. Writes `story-bank`, `tenure-script`,
`showcase-questions`. Needed before any behavioral or deep-dive round.*

**Start from the coverage table, not from the good stories.** Ask which
question shapes have nothing behind them. Left alone, people produce five
versions of the story they most enjoy telling, which is a bank with one story
in it.

For each story, get the situation, the decision, and what changed. Then push:

- **What was rejected.** The most common gap and the first thing an interviewer
  reaches for. An answer presenting the chosen approach as obvious invites the
  follow-up it cannot survive. If nothing was considered, that is the honest
  answer and it goes in as such.
- **The number.** Or the mechanism. Preferably both. `improved`, `streamlined`,
  `optimised` are not weak wording to tighten later — they mark the places
  where the story does not exist yet.
- **The drill sheet.** Every story ends with the follow-ups an interviewer would
  actually ask, aimed at the weak points on purpose: the unverified figure, the
  boundary between the user's work and someone else's, the thing that would
  have broken under ten times the load.

The tenure script is the answer that opens most interviews and the one most
often improvised. Roughly a hundred and thirty words, every number in it
marked, and a prepared answer for each gap or transition it invites.

**Have it said out loud.** A script that reads well and does not speak well is
a script that will be abandoned in the room. Anything that cannot be delivered
without rushing is too long.

For showcase questions, apply one filter: what would the answer change? A
question whose answer changes nothing about whether the user wants the job is
cut, however sharp it sounds.

**Close by testing it.** Have them deliver the tenure script from memory, then
ask one drill-sheet follow-up from a story cold.

## Search

*About thirty minutes. Writes `projects`, `outbound`.*

`projects.md` ranks what the user is prepared to be asked about. Attribution is
already settled, so this stage answers a different question: what to lead with,
and what to steer away from.

**Invert the weak-spot question here.** Not "what are the weaknesses" — ask
which project they would steer an interviewer *away* from, and what they do not
want to be asked about. Those answers are specific and honest in a way the
direct question never gets.

Then push once more, on the part that matters: **what happens if it comes up
anyway.** Everything on that list eventually does, and the difference between a
bad moment and a fine one is whether the answer was decided in advance.

`outbound.md` records targets, routes and the artifact to lead with for each.
The artifact column is the one that pays: a cap makes each daily action scarce,
which is only worth it if the thing attached was chosen for the recipient
rather than reused from the last one.

Confirm the outbound cap value here, in the light of what the targets actually
look like — it was set in the spine stage before any of them were named.

**Close by testing it.** Ask which project they would lead with for the first
target on the list, and why that one.
